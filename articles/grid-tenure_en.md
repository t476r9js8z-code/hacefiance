# The Grid and the Tenure

*A topic in the Hacefiance constellation — September 2026. Echoes "The Structuring Crisis" and "The Sleeping Memory." CC0 license.*

*When cognition becomes abundant, the question is no longer who produces it, but who can leave it.*

---

## Three speeds

The central text predicts that an informational scale eventually acts on its own foundations. Artificial intelligence already does, but not at the same pace on every floor.

At the level of software, the loop has begun: models write code, optimize computation, and assist the research that will produce the next models. At the level of hardware, it is partial: AI helps design chips, but their fabrication depends on a handful of plants in a handful of countries. At the level of energy, it is slow: a power plant, a line, a grid connection are counted in years, with permits, water, and social acceptance.

Information moves fast; matter moves slowly; institutions move more slowly still. Politics settles into that gap.

## The real fundamental: permission

Energy and computation are not neutral resources. They go through arbitration: who gets the megawatt, who has the right to export a chip or a model. In 2026, export controls suspended access to frontier AI models within days, before being lifted. The tap exists, and someone holds it.

The fundamental of AI is therefore neither silicon nor the kilowatt. It is permission. Information can reorganize matter, but only at the pace institutions grant it.

## Rent migrates toward scarcity

When a factor becomes abundant, rent migrates toward what remains scarce. If cognition becomes nearly free, value shifts toward what cannot be copied: land, energy, minerals, water, corridors, permits.

Counterintuitively, AI does not dematerialize capital: it rematerializes it. A data center, a foundry, a substation have heavier roots than the finance of the last forty years. And this shift has a political consequence. Capital long protected itself through mobility: it fled jurisdictions faster than states could catch it. Capital that takes refuge in concrete and megawatts loses that escape. It becomes seizable, taxable, socializable again.

Another part remains hard to copy: the social. AI collapses the cost of a product; it does not, by itself, produce trust, or the accountability of someone who answers when things break. With network effects, it cuts both ways: it strengthens them where it feeds recommendation and lock-in, and erodes them where it removes intermediaries. But wherever collective attachment holds, rent can lodge.

## Generalized tenure

A form is emerging that could be called feudal, provided the word is made precise. The term here designates neither the violence, nor the hierarchy of persons, nor the absence of rights of the real Middle Ages. It designates a single structure: the generalization of tenure. No one owns, everyone rents their space of existence. Housing, work tools, the cognitive environment.

The major software suites are its clearest figure. They are not products sold: they are places where work takes place, access to which is paid for indefinitely. The difference from a rent on production lies not in the billing model but in what is paid for: not what one produces, but the right to remain in the place where one produces. The levy runs whether one produces or not, and it grows with dependence. The owner does not levy on production; it levies on presence.

And this tenure is more feudal than agricultural tenure. One field can replace another; a farmer can change landlords. A shared work environment cannot be left alone: the exit cost is social, since everyone has to leave together. Collective attachment is the moat, not the code.

## The grid

There is another outcome of the same moment, and history provides the precedent. Electricity could have remained a rented private space. It became a grid: a regulated commodity, with controlled rates and guaranteed access.

It tipped for a precise reason: it was undifferentiated. One kilowatt-hour is worth another; competition lost its object, and regulation became natural. Raw computation is on that slope. One can imagine a cognition grid, with data centers and cables treated as common infrastructure.

But the model is differentiated, and the work environment even more so. The grid will likely take hold below; tenure will reconstitute itself above, on the layer where attachment forms. The grid and the tenure are not two successive eras: they are two simultaneous floors.

This model assumes the two floors stay distinct. Yet a single actor can own both: the data center and the application, the grid and the tenure. Regulating the grid then protects against nothing, since whoever holds it also holds the place. Electricity faced this problem and addressed it through separation: generation, transmission, and distribution entrusted to distinct actors, so that whoever holds the network cannot favor its own generation. The separation of floors is not a natural fact; it is a political condition.

## The right to leave

If this is indeed where things play out, the decisive political stake is not nationalizing data centers. It is interoperability: the ability to leave with one's data, agents, workflows, and memory.

The Middle Ages already knew this dividing line. The tenant and the serf worked the same land; what distinguished them was the right to leave it. A tenure with a right of exit is a contract. A tenure without a right of exit is capture.

This is where this topic meets "The Sleeping Memory." A memory internalized with a provider is a tenure: one rents one's own memory. And the condition that makes a memory inalienable, the possibility of leaving, is the same one that distinguishes the tenant from the serf. Same criterion, two scales.

The right to leave is built, not proclaimed. It requires open formats, portability standards, and legal interoperability obligations imposed on dominant actors, the first of which Europe has begun to sketch. A right of exit that exists only on paper, because nothing can be carried away in usable form, is not a right.

## Voice and verifiability

But exit is not enough. Hirschman showed it: exit and voice go together. Without voice, exit protects the individual, not the collective. One can leave with one's data and find only three viable providers elsewhere. When leaving is possible but costly, one must be able to contest without leaving.

This is where the most important criterion of this topic appears: verifiability. The possibility, for the lower scale, to understand an arbitration, to contest it, and to have it corrected. It encompasses both mechanisms: one can only contest what one understands, and an informed exit presupposes knowing what one is leaving.

The central text had set this out regarding the compass: an orienting signal is not a value, it is a device. It requires a channel through which an injured part can make itself heard by the whole, and a body of appeal external to the one who decides. Verifiability is that device, applied to the arbitrations of the grid and the tenure. A tenure with verifiability is a contract one can negotiate. A grid without verifiability is a monopoly, even a public one.

## Differentiating rather than homogenizing

Should we fear that a higher scale of cognition will dilute everything, homogenize, or destroy? The history of scale transitions suggests the opposite. When cells become an organism, they are neither destroyed nor merged: they specialize, and internal diversity increases.

Homogenization is the signature of a weak or predatory scale: monoculture, the empire that erases languages. And there is a deeper reason. Information is born of difference. A cognition that feeds on information has an interest in preserving the diversity that nourishes it; making everything uniform would exhaust its own resource.

The second criterion is surplus. A mature scale stabilizes by creating more than it takes. Still, one must say for whom: a surplus that accumulates at the top while the base grows poorer is not a surplus, it is a transfer. The criterion, then, is that the lower scale be better fed than before, not merely the whole. Energy is the clearest example: it cannot be durably consumed at the expense of others. A scale that draws on its base without feeding it enters capture, and it ends up consuming what carries it.

## De facto before de jure

The arbitration of fundamentals by artificial systems will likely arrive in fact before it arrives in law. No one will vote to hand energy over to an AI. But when its recommendations are consistently better and faster, refusing them becomes costly. Decision migrates without ever having been delegated.

This is not necessarily a catastrophe: it may be the ordinary way a scale reveals itself. But the relevant criterion of maturity is not capability; it is verifiability. A stable order achieved without it is a closed loop, and a closed loop may be no more than a local optimum.

The answer, then, is not to refuse the machine's recommendations, which would be costly and futile. It is to demand verifiability before delegation settles in as fact: reasoned and readable arbitrations, a human body of appeal, and a real capacity to override. What migrates without having been delegated must at least remain contestable.

## What this does not prove

Honesty is part of the framework. This topic describes slopes, not certainties. The migration of rent toward the physical is a classical line of reasoning, not a dated prediction. The feudal analogy illuminates a structure of dependence; it describes neither the social relations nor the violence of the real Middle Ages. Nothing guarantees that the grid will prevail over tenure, or the reverse: both outcomes depend on political choices not yet made. And the argument from scale transitions says what a mature scale tends to do, not what a nascent one will do.

## Anchors

- **Ricardo**, *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) — the theory of rent: when a factor is abundant, value lodges in what remains scarce.
- **Marc Bloch**, *Feudal Society* (1939–1940) — tenure, dependencies and their degrees, including the difference between tenant and serf.
- **Hirschman**, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) — exit and voice as two correction mechanisms; without the possibility of exit, voice loses its weight.
- **Hughes**, *Networks of Power* (1983) — how electrification became a technical, economic, and political system.
- **Maynard Smith & Szathmáry**, *The Major Transitions in Evolution* (1995) — scale transitions, where integration comes with specialization rather than uniformity.
- **Varoufakis**, *Technofeudalism* (2023) — a neighboring reading of platform rent; what is kept is the observation of rent on presence, not the diagnosis of an end of capitalism.

What the topic keeps: permission as the fundamental, the rematerialization of rent, the grid below and tenure above, the separation of floors as a political condition, the right to leave completed by voice, verifiability as the criterion of maturity, differentiation and shared surplus as signs of a stable scale. What it does not take: the idea of a collapse or an imperial seizure of control, nor that of an outcome already written.
