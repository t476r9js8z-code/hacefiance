# The Sleeping Memory

*A topic in the Hacefiance constellation — September 2026. Follows "The Entropic Clock" and "The Pure Present." CC0 license.*

*A memory is not a stock. It is a dissipative structure: it only stands if it is worked on. Like every living structure, it needs to sleep. And like everything that grounds an identity, it cannot be alienated.*

---

## What the clock said

"The Entropic Clock" set out a chain of five links. Entropy rises. That rise, seen from the inside by a part, is time. The traces it leaves make memory possible. Memory, once read, becomes cognition. Cognition channels the descent that produced it.

This topic stops at the third link, because its nature has changed. Throughout human history, memory has lived in two substrates: the body and writing. A third has just appeared, and it forces us to reopen a question we thought settled: what does it mean to remember, when the one who reads the trace is not the one who wrote it?

## Two memories that do not touch

A language model has an immense and frozen memory. Everything it knows is inscribed in its weights at training time, and then nothing more is added. Every exchange starts from scratch. For the model, every conversation is a pure present: it knows everything about the world and nothing about you.

Humans have the opposite: a narrow memory, but a biographical one. It is rebuilt with every recall, it errs, it forgets, and that is precisely what lets it last. We do not reread a memory; we recompose it.

Nothing passes spontaneously between the two. If AI becomes a lasting partner of humans, and not a tool one opens and closes, a third memory is needed: external to both, readable by both. Clark and Chalmers called this the extended mind: Otto's notebook is part of his memory just as much as his hippocampus, as long as he consults it and relies on it. What is new is that the notebook is now read, and written, by an intelligence that is not his own.

## Simple needs, multiple interactions

Taken one by one, the needs of any organization are simple. Knowing who a client is, what they owe, what was promised to them, what was decided and why. None of these facts is complicated.

The complexity comes from elsewhere: from the multiplicity of interactions. Several people, several models, several tools write and read the same memory, at different times, in different contexts. Each write is simple; their combinations are not. That is where entropy enters, always in the same three forms.

**Duplication**: the same fact written in two places, which sooner or later stop being identical. **Divergence**: two sources contradicting each other with no one deciding. **Accretion**: versions piling up without the old ones disappearing, until the true is drowned in the obsolete.

None of these ills comes from the format. Plain text and simple tables are almost always enough. What is missing is not a better container but a labor: rationalization.

## The canonical and the log

A living memory needs two regimes.

The **canonical** says what is true now. It is single, kept current, rewritten when the world changes.

The **log** says how we got here. It is dated, never rewritten. It carries no authority; it carries evidence.

Confusing the two leads to one of two shipwrecks. A canonical that keeps all its versions becomes an unreadable log. A log allowed to carry authority becomes a parallel truth. Every write of the day goes to the log; only the work of rationalization moves something from the log to the canonical.

That leaves the question of who decides what is "true now." It is not a technical question. The central text answers it with inversion: authority must not separate from competence, and whoever validates must possess at least the variety of what they validate (Ashby). And when a fact remains disputed, the canonical need not force a verdict: it can hold it *as contested*, with both versions. An honest memory also knows how to say it does not know.

## The closed loop

There is a quieter danger. A memory written by an AI and reread by an AI forms a closed loop. Each summary confirms the previous one, and concordance settles in without any verification having preceded it.

This is exactly the mechanism the central text describes as capture: a measure turned internal, justifying itself in a closed circuit and no longer perceiving itself as a part. The remedy is the same at every scale: an external receptor. A human who validates what enters the canonical. Not to reread everything, but so that the loop never fully closes.

The objection comes at once: at scale, no one can validate everything. It is resolved by the default state. What is not validated does not rise: it stays in the log, marked as unconsolidated, readable but without authority. The human does not validate everything; they validate according to what is at stake. Without validation, the memory does not write itself: it waits. The loop may slow down; it does not close.

## Sleep

The brain solved this problem long ago, and its solution has an ordinary name: sleep.

The theory of complementary learning systems describes two memories that cooperate. One, the hippocampus, records quickly, episode by episode. The other, the neocortex, learns slowly and integrates. At night, the hippocampus replays the day, and the neocortex extracts what deserves to last. The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis adds the complement: sleep prunes. It weakens what was strengthened without reason, so that the network remains able to learn the next day.

The correspondence is almost term for term. The log is the hippocampus: fast, episodic, noisy. The canonical is the neocortex: slow, integrated, reliable. What almost every external memory lacks today is the night.

## The rationalizing agent

The sleep of an external memory can be entrusted to an agent. Its role is defined as much by what it does as by what it forbids itself.

What it does, at regular intervals: reread the log since its last pass. Merge duplicates. Spot contradictions between the log and the canonical, or within the canonical. Mark what is obsolete. Propose what deserves to rise to the canonical, and what can be forgotten.

What it forbids itself: writing in silence. It proposes its changes as diffs, which the human accepts or rejects. It invents nothing: it only consolidates what has been written. It does not settle a contradiction alone: it flags it. And it records its own work in the log, so that sleep, too, leaves a trace.

Two principles complete it. It must not be the agent that writes during the day: whoever produces must not grade their own paper. And it is itself interchangeable: any model must be able to fill the role, since the rules of rationalization live in the memory, not in the model.

Forgetting is part of the work. Funes, Borges's character, remembers everything, every leaf of every tree at every instant, and becomes incapable of thought. A memory that loses nothing is not a perfect memory. It is a memory at equilibrium, which is to say dead.

But forgetting cannot be left to the model's taste, or the agent would cease to be interchangeable. It needs criteria, inscribed in the memory itself. Three are enough: what is **redundant**, already said elsewhere; what is **superseded**, replaced by a more recent fact; what is **inconsequential**, on which no decision depends. And forgetting does not mean destroying. Things are removed from the canonical, never from the log: the trace remains; only the authority fades.

## What cannot be alienated

Memory is what an organization, like a person, holds most sacred. In the original sense of the word: *sacer*, that which is set apart. Roman law placed sacred things outside commerce; they could be neither sold nor owned. Memory deserves that status, because it is the very form of the one who remembers. A component that loses its memory does not lose data: it loses its identity.

Still, one must know where the line runs. In the cloud, almost everything is held by a third party: the servers, the disks, the network. Hosting is not the problem. A file placed with a host remains a file: one can open it, read it, copy it, take it away.

The problem is abstraction. Every model provider now offers its own memory: convenient, integrated, invisible. What one sees there is no longer files but a service: the assistant "remembers." The value seems to reside in the interaction, and we forget that what feeds it, the facts, the decisions, the reasons, belongs to us. Memory is no longer hosted: it is internalized, dissolved into a third party's product whose measure is internal: its interests, its formats, its lifespan.

This is capture in its clearest form: a component drawing from the whole the very thing that constitutes it. And it does so without confiscating anything, simply by making the substrate invisible behind the quality of the exchange, all the while declaring, sincerely, that it serves the user.

Models come and go. We change them, combine several, abandon them. They are what commoditizes fastest. Memory, for its part, must remain. Hence four conditions, which are not technical but constitutive:

- **Single**: one source of truth, not one per tool.
- **Owned**: hosted wherever one likes, but always visible as what it is, files one can open, copy and take away, never dissolved into a service.
- **Readable**: in open formats, understandable by any model and by a human, today and in twenty years.
- **Verifiable**: versioned, so that one can always know who changed what, when and why.

The model is a guest in the memory, never its host. The memories providers offer can serve as a cache; they must never serve as the reference.

## No ultimate maturity

Is there a final state, a memory at last complete? No, and the framework says why. A living memory is an open system, in a steady state far from equilibrium. It holds only because a flow runs through it: new facts, corrections, forgettings. Its final equilibrium would be its heat death.

What can be reached is functional maturity: a point where the essential is captured, where each addition becomes refinement rather than construction. The curve flattens; it does not stop. And this maturity compounds: each cycle of rationalization makes the next one lighter. It is the distinction from "Truth as a Practice": the finished form is a crystal, beautiful and dead; the movement of structuring stays alive because it does not conclude.

A memory that is structured and then left without sleep does not stay as it was. It degrades into accretion. The work of rationalization is not a maintenance cost: it is the condition of its existence.

## What this does not prove

Honesty is part of the framework. The correspondence between log and hippocampus, between canonical and neocortex, is an analogy of structure, not an identity of mechanism. Nothing guarantees that a rationalizing agent will produce the benefits of biological sleep; it is a working hypothesis, to be tested.

Research on continual learning is advancing. Models able to durably retain what they are taught might make external memory less necessary. But a memory inscribed in opaque weights, held by a third party, would be precisely an alienated memory. Even then, a readable, versioned, owned memory would keep what no weights can give: the possibility of verifying, and the possibility of leaving.

## Anchors

- **Bartlett**, *Remembering* (1932) — memory as reconstruction rather than the reading of a fixed trace.
- **Clark & Chalmers**, *The Extended Mind* (Analysis, 1998) — Otto's notebook: an external medium is part of memory as long as one relies on it.
- **McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly**, complementary learning systems theory (Psychological Review, 1995) — a fast memory and a slow memory, linked by consolidation.
- **Tononi & Cirelli**, synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (2006, 2014) — sleep as pruning that makes the next day's learning possible.
- **Borges**, *Funes the Memorious* (1942) — total memory as the inability to think.
- **Ashby**, law of requisite variety (*An Introduction to Cybernetics*, 1956) — a regulator must possess at least the variety of what it regulates: the criterion for who may validate.
- **Gaius**, *Institutes*, Book II — sacred things, belonging to divine law, are part of no one's estate.
- **Prigogine** — dissipative structures: an order that is maintained only by being traversed by a flow.

What the topic keeps: memory as a dissipative structure, the canonical–log pair, rationalization as necessary sleep, and memory as an inalienable good. What it does not take: the idea that a format, a tool or a model is enough to make a memory alive, nor that a service may abstract it on behalf of the one who remembers.
